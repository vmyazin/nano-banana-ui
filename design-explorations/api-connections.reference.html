Design reference

One place for every key

The connections dialog stops being two lists stacked on top of each other. Each provider gets a single card that says where its key is stored and offers one button to change that. The same dialog becomes the account page’s connection manager.

01

The rule a card follows

Signed in, Save & close puts every key in both places — this device and your account — so cloud jobs work the moment you paste a key. The states below are what is left over: a provider you deliberately kept out, one saved from another device, one you never touched.

DeviceAccountWhat the card saysThe button
—— Nothing. An untouched provider looks exactly as it does today. none
✓✓ On this device · Encrypted in your account — the default, reached without asking for it Remove from account
✓— On this device · Not in your account — only after you opt this provider out Save to account
—✓ Encrypted in your account · ends ··9c1d, plus a line noting browser-only runs need a key here Remove from account

Why the last row matters

It is the row that makes an account worth having. Sign in on a second laptop and the Runware card already knows the account holds a key, instead of looking untouched and inviting you to paste it again. Nothing in the current UI can express this.

The hint is a guard, not decoration

Closing the dialog only writes a key whose last four characters differ from the stored hint. Without that check every close would bump revision — and a bumped revision fails any job still running on the old one.

Removing has to stick

Take a provider out of your account and the next Save & close must not put it back. That is one per-provider flag in the app store, cleared when you press Save to account again.

Deleted

The whole “Saved connections” panel — its provider <select>, its key field, its Save button, its list — along with the “Browser-only connections” heading that only existed to separate it from the grid.

No second step

Paste a key, press Save & close, and cloud jobs run. Nobody has to notice a button to get the thing they signed in for. Five of the seven cards here needed no interaction at all.

One footer sentence

Today the footer explains two storage systems that save on different schedules. Now it states one fact — and it is the disclosure that makes saving-without-asking fair.

05

What this costs

One new component, one file that shrinks by about half, and no backend work — POST /connections without ifAbsent already overwrites and bumps the revision, and DELETE /connections/:provider already exists.

New
components/account/ConnectionStorageControl.tsx

Takes {provider, apiKey, accountId?}. Renders the badge and the button, owns its busy and error state, the POST/DELETE calls, and the removal confirm. Testable on its own.

Edit
components/ApiKeyConfig.tsx

Drop the embedded saved-connections block and the browser-only heading. ProviderCard gains a slot for the control. On close, sync every changed, non-opted-out key to the account.

New
store/useAppStore.ts

One persisted list: the providers you have taken out of your account, so closing the dialog never puts them back.

Cut
components/account/AccountConnections.tsx

The panel variant, the add-a-key form, the provider select and its modal all go. The rail variant survives as a read-only summary with one button.

Edit
components/account/AccountConsole.tsx

Holds the dialog’s open state for the rail.

Out of scope — gating

A provider saved only to the account still reads as not connected in a browser-only workspace, because browser-only genuinely cannot run it. connection-gate-parity passes unchanged.

Out of scope — bulk import

The Provider keys tab in the browser import dialog stays. Per-provider buttons overlap it, but it serves a different moment: migrating everything at once on first sign-in.

The trade accepted

Copy, not move. A key saved to the account also stays on the device, so browser-only keeps working — at the cost of the cleaner “it is in exactly one place” story.
